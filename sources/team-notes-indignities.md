### Westview's Rest Haven
*Event · 1884–1886 · map year 1884*

When Oakland Cemetery ran short of space, the city of Atlanta let a private company open Westview Cemetery in 1884, on the condition that it set apart separate ground for Black burials (pg. 10–11). Westview called that section Rest Haven, after Bishop Gilbert Haven, a white Methodist abolitionist (pg. 11; "God's Acre / Rest Haven"). Westview promised Black families the same treatment, but Rest Haven, like the segregated pauper ground called God's Acre, was left out of the cemetery's 1907 perpetual care plan and fell into neglect (pg. 12; "Party With the Past"; "God's Acre / Rest Haven"). Most of Rest Haven's burials, 191 people, took place between 1884 and 1886 (pg. 12). Sterling Watts, father of South-View founder Albert Watts, was buried there in 1885 (pg. 1). After South-View opened in 1886, Rest Haven burials slowed to a few a year (pg. 12; "God's Acre / Rest Haven"). Westview did not accept Black burials in its main grounds until around 1970; in recent years Rest Haven has been cleaned up and marked with a historical sign ("It's the Largest Cemetery in the South").

### The 1881 Washerwomen's Strike
*Event · 1881 · map year 1881*

After emancipation, most Black women in Atlanta who earned wages washed, cooked, or cleaned for white households (pg. 149; "Atlanta Washerwomen Strike"). In July 1881, about twenty washerwomen met in a church in Summerhill and formed the Washing Society, which set a uniform rate of $1 per dozen pounds of wash ("The Remarkable Story"; "Atlanta Washerwomen Strike"). They went door to door, and within three weeks the society had about 3,000 strikers and supporters, Black and white ("Atlanta Washerwomen Strike"; "We Mean Business"). City authorities fined, threatened, and arrested strikers, and the city council threatened to charge each member a $25 license tax ("Atlanta Washerwomen Strike"; "The Remarkable Story"). The Washing Society wrote to the mayor that its members would pay the fee rather than give up their prices ("We Mean Business"). Accounts differ on how much the strike raised wages, but it showed the city how much it depended on Black women's work ("Atlanta Washerwomen Strike"; "We Mean Business"). Many domestic workers of that era rest at South-View, some under gravestones their white employers put up praising them as "faithful servants" (pg. 149–55).

### Streetcar Segregation and the Boycotts
*Event · 1891–1906 · map year 1892*

In 1891 Georgia became the first Southern state to pass a law segregating streetcars ("The Origins of the Civil Rights Movement in Atlanta"). Black Atlantans answered with a successful strike against the cars in 1892, backed by Black businesses and churches ("The Origins of the Civil Rights Movement in Atlanta"). In 1900 a stricter city ordinance enforced separate seating, and Black Atlantans boycotted again; between 1900 and 1906 Black residents of Atlanta, Augusta, Rome, and Savannah all boycotted their streetcars ("The Origins of the Civil Rights Movement in Atlanta"; "Segregation"). The boycotts won early victories, but white officials kept enforcing segregation, and the protests ultimately failed ("Segregation"). Conflict over seating on crowded streetcars was one of the tensions that built toward the 1906 Atlanta Massacre ("Electrifying Race Relations").

### Wheat Street Becomes Auburn Avenue
*Event · 1893 · map year 1893*

Auburn Avenue began as Wheat Street (pg. 148; "Auburn Avenue (Sweet Auburn)"). In 1893 the city renamed it Auburn Avenue at the request of white petitioners who thought the new name sounded more cosmopolitan ("Auburn Avenue (Sweet Auburn)"; "Sweet Auburn Historic District"). Over the next decades, Jim Crow laws pushed Black businesses out of downtown and onto the avenue, which became the center of Black Atlanta's business, cultural, and church life ("Sweet Auburn Historic District"). Black Atlantans came to call it "Sweet Auburn," a name coined by John Wesley Dobbs ("Auburn Avenue (Sweet Auburn)"). Wheat Street Baptist Church, founded in 1869, kept the street's old name ("Sweet Auburn Historic District").

### The Library That Shut Out Black Atlanta
*Event · 1902–1921 · map year 1921*

When the Carnegie Library of Atlanta opened in 1902, built with a $125,000 gift from Andrew Carnegie, it did not let Black Atlantans use it ("Auburn Branch Library"; "History of AARL"). Carnegie offered $10,000 in 1904 for a branch for Black readers, but neither the city nor the library board acted on it ("Auburn Branch Library"). Black Atlantans kept pressing, and on July 25, 1921, the Auburn Branch opened at 333 Auburn Avenue, the city's first public library for Black residents ("Auburn Branch Library"; "Auburn Avenue Branch, Carnegie Library"). Alice Dugged Cary was its first librarian, from 1921 to 1929 (pg. 187; "History of AARL"). In the 1930s Annie L. McPheeters began collecting books by and about Black Americans there (pg. 228; "Auburn Avenue Branch, Carnegie Library"). The branch closed in 1959, the year Irene Dobbs Jackson received the first card ever issued to a Black Atlantan by the main library ("Auburn Avenue Research Library"; pg. 216). Its collection grew into today's Auburn Avenue Research Library (pg. 228; "History of AARL"). Cary and McPheeters rest at South-View (pg. 187, 228).

2. Griffis, Matthew. "Auburn Branch Library, Atlanta, Georgia (1921–1959)." BlackPast.org, 11 Feb. 2018, https://www.blackpast.org/african-american-history/auburn-branch-library-atlanta-georgia-1921-1959/.

### Unequal Pay for Black Teachers
*Event · 1944–1950 · map year 1944*

For decades the Atlanta Board of Education paid Black teachers and principals on a separate, lower salary schedule than white teachers with the same training and duties ("Samuel L. Davis Papers"; "Cook v. Davis Petition for Rehearing"). Rev. Martin Luther King Sr. pressed for equal pay (pg. 220). In 1944 Samuel L. Davis, a Black teacher, sued the board on behalf of himself and the city's other Black teachers and principals, with NAACP lawyers ("Samuel L. Davis Papers"; "Cook v. Davis Petition for Rehearing"). In 1948 a federal judge ruled against the board's discriminatory pay ("Samuel L. Davis Papers"). The board appealed, and the case continued into 1950 ("Cook v. Davis Petition for Rehearing"). Teacher Rheubin M. Taylor, who later led the Georgia Teachers and Education Association, joined the fight with the Atlanta NAACP; the school system fired him afterward, and though the board reversed the firing, it moved him to an elementary school (pg. 243).

**Sources**

1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 220, 243.
2. "Samuel L. Davis Papers." Robert W. Woodruff Library, Atlanta University Center, finding aid, https://aafa.galileo.usg.edu/repositories/2/resources/37.
3. "Cook v. Davis Petition for Rehearing." 27 Jan. 1950. LDF Archives, Thurgood Marshall Institute, NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund, https://ldfrecollection.org/archives/archives-search/archives-item/04fa4d1a-d597-490c-8b0f-c558766870bf/cook-v-davis-petition-for-rehearing.

### Atlanta's Golf Courses Desegregated
*Event · 1951–1955 · map year 1955*

In 1951 Dr. Hamilton M. Holmes, his sons Alfred "Tup" and Oliver W. Holmes, and a friend were turned away from the city-owned Bobby Jones Golf Course because they were Black ("The Legal Case"; "Alfred 'Tup' Holmes"). The Holmes family sued the city ("The Legal Case"). In 1954 a federal court ruled that shutting them out was discrimination but let the city keep a "separate but equal" arrangement ("The Legal Case"). The NAACP and Thurgood Marshall joined the case, and on November 7, 1955, the U.S. Supreme Court reversed the lower courts in Holmes v. Atlanta ("About the Exhibit"; "The Legal Case"). On December 24 and 25, 1955, the Holmes brothers and their friend played the North Fulton and Bobby Jones courses, desegregating Atlanta's public golf courses ("About the Exhibit"). Their lead lawyer, Roscoe E. Thomas Jr., rests at South-View; Henderson calls the case the first desegregation lawsuit tried in the city (pg. 244).

**Sources**

1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, p. 244.
2. "About the Exhibit." *Holmes v. Atlanta: Changing the Game*, Georgia Institute of Technology, https://sites.gatech.edu/holmesvatlanta/about-the-case.
3. "The Legal Case." *Holmes v. Atlanta: Changing the Game*, Georgia Institute of Technology, https://sites.gatech.edu/holmesvatlanta/legalcase.

### The Color Line at the Fox Theatre
*Event · 1929–1962 · map year 1962*

When the Fox Theatre opened on Peachtree Street in 1929, Black patrons had to use a separate entrance and sit in four rows behind a low concrete barrier in the top balcony ("Remnants of Segregation at the Fox Theatre"). Each spring the Metropolitan Opera performed at the Fox, and Black Atlantans could not get season tickets ("Allen Pledges to Help Negroes Get Met Tickets"). Coretta Scott King, for the interracial Atlanta Committee for Cultural Opportunity, wired Mayor Ivan Allen Jr. about the problem, and he pledged his support ("Allen Pledges"). Dorothy Alexander and Bennie McLendon led the protest to desegregate the opera's performances at the Fox (pg. 170, 228). Opera singer Mattiwilda Dobbs, daughter of John Wesley Dobbs, refused to sing for segregated audiences in her hometown; she first sang in Atlanta in 1962, before an integrated audience at the Municipal Auditorium ("The Legend of Mattiwilda Dobbs"). That same year, two years before the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Fox chose to integrate, and the Met performed Richard Strauss's Elektra for the theater's first integrated audience ("The Fox Theatre at 90"). Dobbs, Alexander, and McLendon rest at South-View (pg. 170, 194, 228).

### Alice Dugged Cary
*Person · 1859–1941 · map year 1921*

Alice Dugged Cary (1859–1941) was born in New London, Indiana, and attended Wilberforce University in Ohio (pg. 187; "Auburn Branch Staff"). In Atlanta she became principal of the Mitchell Street School in the 1880s, the first Black woman to lead a school in the city (pg. 187). She helped organize the first free kindergarten for Atlanta's Black children in 1900, led the high school department and the English department at Morris Brown College, and worked with W.E.B. Du Bois and others to open a public library for Black Atlantans (pg. 187). In 1921 she became the first librarian of the Auburn Branch, the city's first library for Black residents, and ran it until 1929 (pg. 187; "Auburn Branch Staff"). She was also a writer, a juvenile court warden, and a prison reformer, and for many years she gave Atlanta's newsboys a Christmas dinner (pg. 187–88). She rests at South-View.

**Sources**

1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 187–88.
2. "Auburn Branch Staff." *Equal Access: The Unfolding Legacy of the Auburn Branch*, Georgia Libraries, https://georgialibraries.omeka.net/s/AARL/page/staff.

### Annie L. McPheeters
*Person · Died 1994 · map year 1934*

Annie Lou Watters McPheeters (d. 1994) was the first professionally trained Black librarian in the Atlanta Public Library system, with degrees in library science from Hampton Institute and Columbia University (pg. 228). Hired in 1934 as assistant librarian at the Auburn Branch, she became its head two years later and began collecting materials on Black history and culture (pg. 228; "Auburn Avenue Research Library"). In 1949 she moved to run the West Hunter Branch, and in 1966 she became the first Black reference librarian at Georgia State University (pg. 228). The collection she built became the core of the Auburn Avenue Research Library, which names a gallery for her and for Alice Dugged Cary (pg. 228). She rests at South-View.

**Sources**

1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, p. 228.
2. "Auburn Avenue Research Library on African American Culture and History." *New Georgia Encyclopedia*, https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/education/auburn-avenue-research-library-african-american-culture-and-history.

### Mattiwilda Dobbs
*Person · Died 2015 · map year 1962*

Mattiwilda Dobbs (d. 2015), a coloratura soprano and daughter of John Wesley Dobbs, graduated first in her class at Spelman College and studied music in New York and Paris (pg. 194). In 1953 she became the first Black singer to take a principal role at La Scala in Milan, and in 1956 she made her debut at the Metropolitan Opera in New York, which offered her a long-term contract (pg. 195; "The Legend of Mattiwilda Dobbs"). She would not sing before a segregated audience in Atlanta, so her first hometown concert came in 1962, before an integrated audience at the Municipal Auditorium, where Mayor Ivan Allen Jr. gave her the key to the city ("The Legend of Mattiwilda Dobbs"). She rests at South-View.

**Sources**

1. Henderson, D. L. *South-View: An African American City of the Dead*. Carrelspin Press, 2018, pp. 194–95.

## Discrepancies and judgment calls

1. **Teacher-pay plaintiff.** Henderson (pg. 243) tells the story through Rheubin M. Taylor. The court records and the AUC finding aid name **Samuel L. Davis** as the plaintiff. The card names Davis as plaintiff and keeps Taylor's role (and firing) as Henderson tells it.
2. **Teacher-pay outcome on appeal.** The 1948 district ruling went against the board, but the board appealed to the Fifth Circuit (Cook v. Davis, 178 F.2d 595, 1949), and the LDF petition for rehearing is dated Jan. 27, 1950. I could not confirm how the appeal ended, so the card stops at "the case continued into 1950." Worth a check before anyone adds more.
3. **Auburn Branch dates.** The HMDB marker's inscription says the library was on the corner "from 1923 to the 1950s"; BlackPast, the AARL history, and the marker page's own notes say it opened July 25, 1921, and closed in 1959. The card uses 1921 and 1959.
4. **Cary's name.** The Auburn Branch marker spells it "Alic Dugged Carey"; Henderson, AARL, and a nearby marker spell it "Alice Dugged Cary." The card uses Cary.
5. **Cary at Morris Brown.** The marker calls Cary "the first principal of Morris Brown College"; Henderson (pg. 187) says she led its high school and English departments. The card follows Henderson.
6. **Rest Haven after 1886.** Henderson and the HMDB marker say Rest Haven burials fell to a few a year after South-View opened; the AJC (Oct. 2026) says burials there continued into the early 20th century. Both can be true; the card says burials "slowed."
7. **Washerwomen numbers and outcome.** NMAAHC gives about 3,000 strikers and supporters (some accounts count only strikers). Sources differ on how much wages rose, so the card does not claim a specific win.
8. **Streetcar boycotts.** The AHA calls the 1892 strike successful; the New Georgia Encyclopedia says the 1900–1906 boycotts ultimately failed. The card gives both, in order.
9. **Golf "first."** Henderson (pg. 244) calls Holmes v. Atlanta the first desegregation lawsuit tried in the city. I found no outside confirmation, so the card attributes the claim to Henderson. The fourth golfer is left unnamed because I could not confirm he has died.
10. **Fox Theatre.** Wikipedia calls the Fox the only Atlanta theater that admitted both Black and white patrons; the AJC says "one of the few." The card makes neither claim. Henderson credits Dorothy Alexander and Bennie McLendon with leading the protest; the Ivan Allen records credit Coretta Scott King and the Atlanta Committee for Cultural Opportunity. The card names all three. Atlanta Magazine dates the Fox's first integrated audience (Met, *Elektra*) to 1962.
11. **Wheat Street rename date.** One list gives April 17, 1893; the NGE gives only 1893. The card says 1893.
12. **Card title rule.** The existing "Pittsburgh Riot" card still conflicts with the house rule ("massacre," not "riot"). Not changed here; needs a team decision.
